Lately, I have noticed something interesting: **contributing on GitHub really can be addictive.**

At first, I thought it was just the novelty. Find an interesting issue and see whether I can fix it. If I can, claim it, create a branch, change the code, submit a PR, run CI, and wait for the author's review. If it gets sent back, keep working until CI turns green, the author approves it, and the code is merged.

Before long, though, I find myself browsing the issue list again. Is there anything new I could pick up today? Sometimes I check several times a day.

Eventually, I started wondering why some open-source projects had left me completely indifferent, while this one kept pulling me back.

I do not think GitHub itself has any particular magic. I happened to find my way into **an exceptionally good engineering environment**.

---

## An engineering environment that keeps pulling me back

This project has an excellent approach to collaboration. The issue list keeps moving, so there is always another problem to work on. Releases happen quickly, too, almost every day.

That matters a lot to me. Your code does not sit on the main branch after being merged, waiting for some unknown release date. It can be in the next day's release. You get a very tangible sense that **someone is already using what I wrote yesterday**.

Even more valuable is the review culture. The author and core contributors are in the group almost every day, and PRs do not get left unattended. Flaws in the code or weaknesses in the design get sent back for reworking without hesitation.

Honestly, by the third round of revisions, it can be frustrating. Strangely, though, I also find it satisfying.

Because it means **someone is finally taking a serious look at what I write**.

---

## Becoming the team's final source of technical judgment

That experience cast a different light on how I had been working before.

In my previous team, I put together roughly 90% of the architecture guidelines, documentation, and technical standards. I made the key technical decisions. The final review before code was merged usually came down to me as well.

Over time, I became the most visible point in the system from which guidance flowed in just one direction.

People came to me with difficult problems, handed me proposals to review, and waited for my decisions when incidents happened. Of course, that reflected the team's trust in me. It also showed that the experience I had accumulated over the years was useful.

But there was a cost: **I kept giving, while the input I received became increasingly scarce**.

It was not that I had stopped learning. The everyday work had simply come close to exhausting its ability to challenge me. More and more situations were no longer, “I do not know how to do this; let me figure it out.” They were, “I have dealt with this before; I will handle it.”

I became more practiced at what I did, but those moments of “I never realized you could write it this way” almost disappeared. The most unsettling question was this: I set the standards and approved the designs. If my own judgment had blind spots, **who would review me?**

---

## Professional loneliness

I once came across the term **professional loneliness**, and it felt remarkably precise.

Professional loneliness does not mean there is nobody around you. Quite the opposite: I used to spend my days in countless meetings and group chats, surrounded by colleagues I enjoyed working with.

But the feeling was closer to this: **I had a team, but I was missing peers**.

There were too few people around me who could challenge me professionally and point out where I was wrong.

When I proposed a design, rarely would someone ask, “Why does it have to work this way? Would another approach perform better?” I reviewed other people's code, but rarely did anyone point out the flaws in mine.

Staying in that position for too long creates a quiet kind of exhaustion. You still love technology and enjoy building things, but you know your reserves are constantly being drawn down, with almost nobody helping replenish them.

---

## Work draws on experience; open source replenishes it

Looking back, almost all the input that truly excited me over the years happened outside work.

Building side projects, keeping up with new tools and technologies, studying how outstanding teams approach engineering, or even doing what I am doing now: submitting my code to a maintainer I have never met and letting them pick it apart.

On the surface, it looks like I am doing even more tiring coding. But the experience is completely different:

**At work, I am often drawing on experience I already have. In a high-caliber open-source project, the environment pushes me to grow.**

At work, I was the person used to saying, “I know how to do this.” But in a strong community, when strict CI checks block my code or a maintainer rejects my approach, I rediscover the excitement of saying, “I had no idea you could handle it this way.”

Sometimes I even enjoy having a PR sent back for revisions.

Being examined and having my judgment tested by people at my level, or beyond it, had become far too rare in my everyday work.

---

## Away from the office, I found peers again

This experience also strengthened my decision not to return to an office job in the near term.

I used to think that staying technically sharp meant remaining inside an established organization, leading a team, and taking responsibility for the business. But I eventually realized that workplaces often give you colleagues with their own responsibilities rather than a community of technical peers. Everyone's main concern is shipping requirements on time and keeping the business running. Few people have the energy or inclination to examine whether the code is as good as it could be.

After leaving the office, I found a much purer kind of connection.

The maintainers who suggest improvements on GitHub, sometimes sending my PR back three times in a row, have no financial relationship with me. We have not even met. Yet professionally, they are the people who take my work most seriously and hold me to the highest standard.

What makes you lose touch is not leaving an office. It is stagnating in a place where your knowledge only ever flows outward.

I am not missing a job, or colleagues.\
**I am missing peers.**

It turns out that peers do not have to share an office. Even without a desk or an employment contract for now, at least in the world of code, I have rediscovered the care and quiet confidence that come with taking your craft seriously.
